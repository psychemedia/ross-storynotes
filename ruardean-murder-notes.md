# Frances Bennett Notes

"for about the last eight years, according to her own statement, had this unnatural and monstrous woman been almost yearly in the constant practice of giving birth to and murdering a child without incurring any suspicion; and it was only from the compunctions of her own overladen guilty conscience, in what she no doubt supposed to be the dread hour of death, that the horrid tragedy above narrated was disclosed to the world"

Frances ("Fanny") Bennett

- ran brewhouse
- between forty and fifty years of age, at least 10 years older than Yapp
- well furnished cottage, Ruardean Woodside; every appearance of comfort
- widow of a man named Bennett
  - left her his property
  - if ever she married again the property should pass from her (to his children?)

- cohabit with Yapp for for 8-10m even 12 years
- had been ill for a twelve months or so; now very ill, "consumptive variety" (so: weight loss, fatigue, fever; post mortem by Mr Hickes, surgeon, identified pulmonary consumption, so, TB), confined to bed for a week, fearing death, confessed in a state of delirium to Formby (1); rumour circulated that she might be pregnant again.
- (1) 12 months previously killed child a few days old by poisoning, buried it beneath pavement in brewhouse
- (2) also five other children murdered at birth
- skeletons of six children found; most recent grave indicated concealed "the most perfect skeleton".
- Yapp arrested
- policeman remained with Bennett, who confessed again to her sister, then to policeman (Fowler)
- seem to recover somewhat and at inquest (Tues, Nov 1st, 1842) denied giving birth, denied confessing to priest, or sister, or to policeman; but illness returned and considered such she would not survive; clergyman was a witness; person who had found the remains of the bodies;
- coroner forbade anyone to enter her sick room except sister and two policeman, who stayed with her day and night from Nov 1st; Formby appealed against this and relaxed on Weds Nov 9th at second coroner's hearing, which was adjourned to Weds 16th in hope more evidence might be gathered; some say curate had a second interview, alone, with Bennett, in which she made additional disclosures, which may or may not have been at this time; what is known is that on Friday 11th, magistrate's clerk, a county magistrate, and the superintendent took Yapp to see her, demanded repetition on oath in his presence of all previous statements (for the avowed purpose of taking away his life), which she absolutely denied; on which point Coroner reinforced original provisions;
- third meeting of Coroner's court, Wednesday, 16th November, 1842; several additional witnesses corroborated previous evidence, but one with new evidence — Watkins — after which, jury found a verdict of "Wilful murder", and both prisoners committed for trial at next Assizes.
- Yapp then taken to County Gaol in Gloucester, and Bennett given medical examination then taken there too, attended by a medical man, Mr Bird, very debilitated / reduced to a skeleton but her features more pleasing than might otherwise expect, and unchanged from three weeks before, and not expected to live till her trial.
- in gaol, Bennett attended by Elisa Gansmor, one of nurses attached to gaol, commented Bennett "thanked God that she had been removed, and that she was much happier in gaol than at home", also that she was only 38 years of age. Was aware she had not long to live. Died after 8 days in gaol early in the morning, (Fri 2nd Dec 1842?), Coroner (Inquest Sat 3rd Dec. 1842) mentioned rumour of pregnancy and asked Gansmor if she was in family way (she wasn't). Maria Nuttal, another nurse, present at death, and was asked to read to and pray to Bennett ("Stop; wait till I'm better," and when she recovered she would ask her to go on); contented at end, sensible to last. Verdict: "Died from natural causes"; Yapp wept bitterly on hearing news

- died

Thomas Yapp

- c. 34 years of age, illiterate and sullen man, collier, a labourer of the better class, but also claimed as recently being a `hallier`, which is to say, *haulier*;
- arrested after initial confession
- sent into Bennett's death chamber to try to get a death bed confession of what *he* had done ("the cause of all her crimes"), but she said nothing
- Spring Assizes, April 1843: pomp and ceremony around arrival of High Sherriff: carriage drawn by four horses of remarkable beauty, richly decorated with silver trimmings on a dark ground; harness similarly ornamented, coachman and two footmen in handsome liveries. High Sheriff met a few miles from his estate, by about forty of his tenantry, who formed a procession, and rode in front of carriage. Javelin men, in white and blue livery, leading cavalcade, and attended by a large number of citizens behind, proceeded along Monmouth road till meeting Mr. Sergeant Talfourd, bearer of the royal commission—their Lordships being detained at Monmouth by the pressure of business. On arriving in Gloucester,  Learned Sergeant proceeded to Shire-hall, and her Majesty's commission was read.
- (Judge remarked in his opening address that out of the 110 prisoners whose names were in the calendar when I first saw it, I found that 100 of them could neither read nor write, or at best but imperfectly)
- No Bill form Grand Jury, but there was a charge arising from Coroner's court, that Yapp was an accessary to wilful murder of several infant children; Mr. Francillon, prosecution, stated after an anxious consideration of case, no evidence to offer and Yapp discharged.

Rev Henry Formby

- Anglican/Protestant curate at Ruardean
- admitted woman had made a confession to him, *and another*, but he could not divulge contents (not the law in Anglican church, though it is in Roman Catholic);
- coroner said at second hearing tried to persuade him to reveal more, but then said he would "leave the matter as it stands"; but it would surely need revisiting...
- letter:  The unhappy mother, on the morning in question, made known the details of her crime to her neighbours. The rumour spread, and attracted me from a neighbouring district to the spot. Before entering the woman's house, I inquired the leading details in a neighbour's cottage, and from the bystanders; and, on afterwards entering, I heard their report confirmed in the manner stated by me in evidence. Whilst I was in the room some person or persons unknown entered and busied themselves in it. So far, then, nothing transpired of a confidential character. Had I thought so, I should not have been at liberty to have spoken at all ...  My subsequent interviews with the unhappy offender were strictly those of the clergyman and the penitent, and therefore sacred.... [Neither conform nor deny] It has been pointed out to me that the report in the *Gloucester Chronicle* states that further disclosures had been made to me. Such a notion, however probable, can of course be no more than inferential. For all that is or can be known, it may be without foundation.
- resigned three years later (as did another everend also working around Ruardean) and then joined Roman Catholic Church

Sister of Frances Bennett

- lives next door
- never suspected pregnancy, nor any other neighbouring females;
- with sister Sat Oct 29th, 1842;
- before curate came, admitted:
  - "have my dead children dug up and buried in the church-yard"
  - delivered herself of children over a pan of water;
  - immediately the infants were born held them under the water till they were dead;
  - she and Yapp buried them; two near a barn-door belonging premises, two under the brewhouse, two in another part of the premises (under paving in kitchen?); at period of one of murders mother kept an ale-house, and used sign as part of coffin; heard it said that five skeletons were found ecactly where she said, but not sixth: "Oh, I remember, the pigs eat him!"
  - last of the six children lived for two days; weakly child; gave it arsenic and it died; Yapp sorry when found this child dead, said he would not have lost it for fifty pounds; they both together buried the child in the brewhouse

Elizabeth Yapp, a sister of Thomas Yapp, residing at same place:

- a month after the death of Bennett, her sister was back in coroner John Cooke's court on rumour she had killed her own illegitimate child. Child had lived -6 weeks (born mid. December?) and taken care of as well as wretched circumstances allowed. "Died from natural causes." Born before baptised, and application made (as was custom) to sexton to bury it privately; but given circs and rumoir of negligence or misconduct of mother, sent to Coroner.
- very poor, did what she could

John Cooke, Esq, Coroner, one of County Coroners

- following the case, turns out Cooke had consulted Mr. Bellamy, CLerk of Assizes, who had in turn consulted a barrister, Mr, Francillon to prepare the inquisitions for the final coroner court hearing, which was paid for and the Coroner claimed as an expense, which was later objected to.

Watkins - (3rd coroner's court hearing): made acquaintance of Yapp around about previous Chirstmas, was in neighbourhood of Ruardean, sinvited to spend night and stay at Yapp & Bennett's. Slept in same room / bed with Yapp, Bennett in adjoining room. From 12 midnight till 2 or 3, sounds of person in great pain in next room kept him awake. Woke at 5, and Yapp went downstairs for a light/look for matches, but couldn't find any. Yapp came back upstairs and into Bennett's room but Watkins fell back asleep. Returned to neighbourhood previous Saturday, called on Bennett, who asked "do you remembered sleeping there last Christmas?" — he did— "I deceived you nicely that night, for that was the very time when my last child was born." / "Ah, I tricked you all very nicely that night. That was the time when I had my last child, but I kept it very snug, and none of you knew anything about it."
