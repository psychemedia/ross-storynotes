# Murders at the Rose-in-Hand

Life in and around the Forest was often a physical one, working as colliers in the mines, or labouring on the roads. In the surrounding areas, many worked on the land.

The following reports, of a fatal fight in Clanna, to the east of the Forest, describes not only the tragic incident of a death resulting from a brawl, but also gives a flavour of the times, with a vivid description of bringing in the corn harvest.

COLEFORD. 

FATAL FIGHT.— On the 11th inst. a fight took place between Walter Lewis and James Davis, labourers, of Woollastone. Lewis and Davis, with seven others, had been reaping for the Hon. William  M. Noel, on the Clanna estate, when several them angry words passed between them during the day respecting their work. About eight o'clock in the evening they were both intoxicated, having drunk freely during the day out of a cask of cider, which they had purchased that day from their employer, who lets the reaping for a fixed price, and the reaper finds his own drink. They continued quarrelling, when Lewis called Davis "Mr. Ludlow." Davis said if he called bim that again be would knock him down; when Lewis in reply said “Will you, Mr. Ludlow?" Upon this, Davis threw down his reaping hook, rushed at Lewis, and struck him, and regular stand up fight took place, two of their comrades acting as seconds. One named Wm. Evans, acted as Davis's, and Lewis's brother as his. James Davis was 43 years of age, and a more powerful man than his adversary, but he was drunk and determined. Lewis is about 24 years of age, and had by far the worst of the fight for the first half hour; in the last round Davie was making for his opponent, when he missed him, aad Lewis struck him a severe blow on the right side of the face near the neck, which felled him to the ground, and it is said that Davis's left temple came in contact with a stone. When his second, Evans, went to pick him up he said, "That's a finisher," and never spoke afterwards. He was at once picked up and conveyed to the house of a labourer named George Howell, who lived near. Some time after, Mr. Tuckwell, surgeon, of Aylburton, was sent for, and on the next morning Davis was taken to his home, where he expired at three o'clock on the morning of the 13th inst. Shortly after his death information was given to the police at Lydney, and Sergeant Webb and P.C. Huntly went to Clanna and apprehended Walter Lewis. On the following day Sergeants Webb and Banning apprehended William Evans and Edmund Lewis, labourers, of Woollastone, (the seconds.) An inquest was held on the body on the 15th inst. before J. Barnett, Esq., at the Globe Inn, Alvington; and after one witness had been examined, the inquiry was adjourned until the 28th instant, in order that a *post mortem* examination may be made on the body. The three prisoners, who were present during the inquiry, were removed by the police to Whitemead Park, before E. Machen, Esq. who bound them over in £40. each, and three sureties in £20. each, to appear before the coroner, at the Globe Inn, Alvington, on the 28th instant.

FOREST OF DEAN.

The Fatal Fight at Clanna.— We gave brief notice this sad occurrence in our paper of Saturday week. Our readers will recollect that, on the 11th ult., two labourers, named James Davies and Walter Lewis, both of the parish of Woollastone, were reaping with four or five others in a field at Clanna, belonging to the Hon. Davis said 'Call me Ludlow again and I will do it;' I then said 'Will you, Mr. Ludlow?' Davis then throwed down his hook and run at me, and struck me first; we then fought a long time; William Evans seconded Davies, and my brother Edmund picked me up; Davis was drunker than any man in the field; I had not a fair chance to strike Davies until the last round; my brother Edmund held me down on his knee; I had not a chance to get on my legs before Davis knocked me down; I told my brother before the last round to let me get on my legs and be ready; Davis came up and struck at me, but did not hit me; I then struck him in the face and hit him staggering; I followed him and gave him a blow with my left hand on the side of his neck and knocked him down; I should think the blow I gave him did not kill him; I should have given Jones something that morning if it had not turned out as it have, but I will do it some other time."—George Edmonds, of the parish of Alvington, labourer, deposed: On Monday evening, the 11th inst. I was standing at my garden gate when the two Lewises passed along the road; I heard one say "Ludlow thought of being master to night, but he got took in; Mr. Evans thought he was a fancy man, but if he hadn't mind what he said he would have got it;" next morning, Rich. Bowing, the Hon. W. M. Noel's bailiff, told me that Jas. Davis was almost killed; I then informed Mr. Bowing of what I heard the night before.—Mr Tuckwell, surgeon, of Aylburton, deposed: I saw the deceased on the 12th inst. at the house of George Howell, lying on a bed or mattress, insensible to sound, but when I bled him he moved his arm from the touch of the lancet, gaped twice, and put his hand over his chest; I then had some slight hopes that he might recover; a messenger came to me on the following day to say he was dead; when lying in the cottage deceased had a peculiar blackness over his eyes and cheek, and a bruise on the forehead, apparently from a fall; there were marks of fingers on the inside of both arms, as though he had been held back; I stayed with the deceased from half an hour to three quarters, and left the quite insensible: I could not tell how the insensibility was caused; on the Friday morning I proceeded, in consequence of the direction given to me by Mr. Barnett, and made a *post mortem* examination; I first opened the head; on removing the skin of the skull I observed slight effusions of blood, corresponding with the bruised skin externally, over the left eyebrow; there was no fracture of the skull; on removing the skull cap, I found an enormous effusion of blood, and also effusion beneath the outer membrane of the brain; the effusion was the result of several small veins breaking between two membranes—the *dura mater* and *pia mater*; to this effusion I attribute the death.—The jury returned a verdict of "Manslaughter" against Walter Lewis, and a similar verdict against Edmund Lewis, who were both committed under the coroner's warrant for trial at the next Gloucester assizes.

At Newnham Police-court, on Monday, Thomas Buffin, William Phillips, *alias* Crow, and Joseph Bidmead, of Drybrook, were charged on remand with being drunk and riotous, and assaulting P.C.s Wood and Walcroft. The case, which occupied several hours in its hearing, attracted considerable interest.

9th January 1890

THE FOREST OF DEAN MYSTERY.

RESUMPTION OF THE INQUEST.

The inquiry into the circumstances of the death of Joseph Tyler, a middle-aged labourer, of Nailbridge, near Drybrook, who was found dead in the Morse-lane, in the parish of Ruardean, on the night of Christmas Day, was for the third rime resumed on Wednesday evening at The National School, Drybrook.— Proceedings commenced with reading over to the witnesses of the evidence given by them at the previous hearing.

Mary Carmock, with whom the deceased lodged, added to what she had already stated that she was not aware that deceased sewed money in his clothing. He was usually possessed of three purses; one was found on the body and the other two in her house. Witness was perfectly satisfied she had never heard that deceased had fits.

Thomas Trigg, re-examined, said deceased's head as he lay on the ground was towards Ruardean. Witness told the magistrates at Littledean that prisoner said, "I fancy I saw someone run away from here." He would not now take his oath whether or not the word "fancy" was expressed by prisoner.

Timothy Marfell said when prisoner made the remark about someone having run from the body he went back and discovered Moses Matthews and his wife 70 yards away.

John Marfell, re-examined, said his brother, the previous witness, remarked to the prisoner, "There is foul play here," to which the prisoner replied, "I saw someone run away." Witness knew nothing more material to the case.

By a Juryman: It was after a light was struck that the prisoner said he did not know who the man on the ground was.

Mr. Goold remarked that this was a flat contradiction to the evidence of the previous witness who had said Mansell's statement about "not knowing the deceased" was used before the light was struck.

By the Foreman: Witness, who knew the deceased, did not recognise him till after the light was struck; it was dark.

Richard Mansell, the prisoner's brother, re-examined, said the prisoner when witness last saw him was going towards Drybrook. He could not now say that he had left the deceased in the house, which he had previously sworn to. His explication was that he was not much of a scholar.

By the Foreman: Witness was at his father's house when he first heard of the death of the deceased. That was the morning of Boxing Day. He had said he was unwilling for his brother to get into a bother that night because there were words between him and man called Wills.

Moses Matthews, re-examined, said he passed the prisoner and the deceased in the road within four feet. A voice said, "Get up; the police will be here."

By the Foreman: Witness could see anyone twenty yards off. He was with his wife. They did not halt, but kept going on.

Mrs. Myra Matthews, wife of the last witness, said she did not hear either of the men speak at all. She heard no one speak. She then met four men, and in the space of a very short distance two of the four men returned and spoke to witness.

Police-constable Price, re-examined by the jury, said deceased's hands were not scratched or dirty. [His clothing was here produced, the jury desiring to examine it.] The right side of the coat was very dirty, and there were blood stains on the dirt. Deceased's stick was covered with mud. His trousers were dirty along the entire length of the right leg. Deceased's hair and cap were clotted with mud and blood. His cap was intact, except that there were blood and mud on it.
 
Superintendent Ford spoke to the finding of the stone that fitted the wound on the deceased's head. There was a pool of blood where the stone was found. There were no signs of a struggle.

Mr. Goold set up a theory that everything tended to show that it was a case of accidental death, and that the prisoner's conduct throughout was consistent with that theory. The fact that George Mansell's clothing was not soiled was pointed to as being in his favour. One point not raised which he (the coroner) would draw attention to, in fairness to Mansell, was the improbability that a person would choose Christmas night to commit a murder on the Queen's highway. It would almost appear that Mansell and Tyler fell from the clouds on to the road where they were found, as no one seemed to be able to trace them. Notwithstanding a contrary statement, it was patent that Mansell did see and was in the company of the deceased on the fateful night. Mansell suggested that Tyler was drunk. Why, then, did he not ask Matthews to help him up? The more important point was, when Mansell discovered that Tyler was really dead, he dropped the drunkenness theory and suggested that someone had run from the body. The jury must draw conclusions from that. What occasion was there to run away from a drunken person? It was an attempt on the part of the guilty person to cast suspicion on another party. The jury might say that death was accidental or wilful; that Mansell did it, or that the evidence was not clear enough to show who did it.

The jury were absent 40 minutes, and on their return brought in a verdict "That the deceased was killed by a blow from a stone, but what person or persons inflicted the fatal blow the evidence was not sufficient to show." The Foreman added that the evidence in some respects was unsatisfactory, a great deal being withheld that might have been adduced. If anyone was worse than another it was the witness Burton.
